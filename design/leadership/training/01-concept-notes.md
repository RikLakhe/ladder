## 1. Cognitive bias literacy (PF 1)

The entire bias thread of Decision Making — from explaining bias at P2, to reflecting on personal bias at P3, to raising team-wide bias-awareness at P5 — requires actually knowing what biases look like, not just that "bias exists." A few worth knowing by name: confirmation bias (favoring information that supports what you already believe), anchoring (over-weighting the first number or option you see), and sunk-cost reasoning (continuing something because of what's already been invested, not what it's worth going forward).

**You need this when:** you notice you've already decided something before you've actually looked at the evidence.

## 2. Accountability frameworks (PF 1)

Owning an outcome is different from owning only the effort put in. "I tried hard" explains an outcome; it doesn't take accountability for it. Accountability means treating the outcome — good or bad — as yours to answer for, and changing your approach based on it.

**You need this when:** a decision you made didn't work out, and the easy move is to explain why it wasn't really your fault.

## 3. Organizational strategy literacy (PF 1)

"Conversations based on organizational strategy" require a real strategy to reference, not an assumed one. See `../../my-role.md` and `../../goals/company-okr-2026.md` for the concrete, current artifacts this competency's strategy language traces to. This concept note is shared grounding with Strategic Impact's equivalent Required Knowledge item — the same literacy underlies both competencies, applied here to decision-making and alignment rather than business/product judgment.

**You need this when:** someone asks "how does this connect to strategy?" and the honest answer would be a guess rather than a reference to something real.

## 4. Change management and process-improvement methodology (PF 1)

Discussing a process improvement is different from landing one. Effective change usually means proposing something specific (not just naming a problem), piloting it on a small scale before rolling it out broadly, and measuring whether it actually helped — needed specifically for the P6 delta where Process Thinking shifts from discussion to driving implementation.

**You need this when:** a process complaint has been raised more than once without anything actually changing.

## 5. Goal-setting and OKR mechanics (PF 1)

"Ensuring continuous work towards shared goals" requires an actual method for checking progress — knowing how goals cascade from company to team, and how to tell whether current work is actually tracking towards one, not just a felt sense that things are generally fine.

**You need this when:** you're several weeks into a project and haven't checked whether it's still pointed at the goal it was supposed to serve.

## 6. Meeting facilitation techniques (PF 2)

The mechanical toolkit underneath "ensures everyone has an opportunity to share their opinion" and "ensures no one person dominates": turn-taking (explicitly inviting quieter voices), time-boxing (allocating and enforcing time per topic), and a parking lot (a visible place to defer off-topic points without losing them).

**You need this when:** the same one or two people are talking in every meeting and you haven't yet tried a structural fix, just a hopeful one.

## 7. Group decision-making methods (PF 2)

Not every discussion needs full consensus. Consent (no one has a blocking objection), a decider-model (one person owns the call after hearing input), and true consensus (everyone actively agrees) are different tools for different stakes — needed for the P6 delta, where facilitation must guide a discussion toward an actual decision with buy-in, not just balanced airtime.

**You need this when:** a discussion has surfaced good input from everyone but still hasn't converged on a decision.

## 8. Reading group dynamics (PF 2)

A facilitator who can't read the room can't act on what this row asks for — noticing disengagement (people going quiet, checking out), dominance (one person crowding out others), or unspoken disagreement (nodding without actually agreeing) in real time, not after the fact.

**You need this when:** a meeting felt like it went fine, but the actual outcome didn't stick afterward — that gap is often a dynamic you missed in the room.

## 9. Cross-team stakeholder mapping (PF 2)

"Ensuring relevant parties are included" (P6) assumes you already know who "relevant" means in a given cross-team context — which requires having mapped, in advance, who actually has a stake in or informs the outcome.

**You need this when:** a cross-team decision gets made and someone who should have weighed in finds out about it afterward.

## 10. Mentoring versus managing distinction (PF 3)

Mentoring is developmental and non-evaluative — it doesn't carry the authority or stakes of a management relationship. Without this distinction, "mentors teammates" risks sliding into unsolicited management, especially once mentoring extends beyond one's own team where you have no formal standing at all.

**You need this when:** you catch yourself telling a mentee what to do rather than helping them work out what they think they should do.

## 11. Psychological safety in mentoring relationships (PF 3)

The LFT matrix names the manner explicitly at P3/P4: "open, respectful, flexible, empathetic." A mentoring relationship where the mentee feels judged, or where the format doesn't flex to what they actually need, won't produce real development regardless of how much time is spent.

**You need this when:** a mentee seems to be telling you what they think you want to hear, rather than what's actually going on.

## 12. Succession and redundancy planning basics (PF 3)

Bus-factor reduction (making sure more than one person can do something critical) and backfill readiness are the concrete goals behind the P4 delta, where mentoring gains a deliberate strategic purpose beyond general development.

**You need this when:** you realize a piece of team knowledge lives in exactly one person's head.

**Further reading (fetched live this session):** staffeng.com's discussion of the Tech Lead archetype's delegation practice — https://staffeng.com/guides/staff-archetypes — touches on this indirectly: delegating complex work is partly how redundancy gets built.

## 13. Coaching-the-coach (PF 3)

Helping someone else become a better mentor is a distinct meta-skill from being a good mentor yourself — needed specifically for the P7 delta, "supports others in their growth as mentors." It requires observing someone else's mentoring practice and giving them feedback on it, which is a step removed from mentoring directly.

**You need this when:** you're at P7 and the honest answer to "how are you scaling mentoring" is "I'm still doing most of it myself." This is the exact gap Guided Exercise 14 is built to close — it's the only training unit that produces `LEAD-3-P7`'s mandatory coaching-the-coach evidence (see `05-badge-reference-card.md`).

## 14. Culture-building mechanics (shared across PF 1 and PF 3)

How does a personal habit become a team, cross-team, or organizational norm? Rituals (a recurring practice with a fixed slot), modeling (visibly doing the thing yourself, consistently), and incentives (making the behavior easier or more rewarded than its absence) are the three main levers. Every P5+ Performance Criterion involving "fosters a culture" across this competency's PF 1 (alignment culture) and PF 3 (mentoring culture) is fundamentally about pulling one or more of these levers.

**You need this when:** you're at P5+ and the honest answer to "how did you foster this culture" is "I modeled it myself a lot" — that's modeling without the ritual or incentive structure to make it durable once you're not doing it personally anymore. Note that a culture/adoption claim built on this note's mechanics still needs the §1.6 three-part adoption artifact (named contact, dated reference, what changed) to actually clear a badge's completion bar — the mechanics explain how to build the culture; §1.6 is how you prove it happened.

## 15. Client-constrained improvement navigation (PF 1)

At LFT, "improving practices and processes" almost never happens on a blank internal canvas. On any given account, the client's contract type, existing tooling, and governance decide what you can change directly (usually your own team's internal engineering habits), what you have to negotiate (anything touching client-owned tooling, client process, or client-visible deliverables), and what you simply have to work around (anything the client has explicitly mandated and won't move on). Confusing these three categories — trying to "fix" something that's actually a negotiation, or giving up on something that was actually changeable — is the most common failure mode here.

**You need this when:** you've spotted a real quality gap, and the client's own process or tooling is part of why it exists. This is the theory behind Guided Exercise 9, which builds `LEAD-1-P5`'s mandatory, non-substitutable client-constraint evidence item — see `05-badge-reference-card.md`.

## 16. Facilitating across the LFT-client boundary (PF 2)

Facilitating a ceremony or working session with client stakeholders in the room is a different exercise from facilitating a purely internal meeting: you don't have organizational authority over the client attendees, the client side may have its own internal politics you can't see, and a client decision-maker in the room can end a discussion by simply outranking it rather than by the room actually converging. Good facilitation here means applying the same balanced-participation and goal-tied-outcome standard to the client side of the room as to the LFT side, and explicitly checking for buy-in rather than accepting agreement that's actually just deference to rank.

**You need this when:** a client stakeholder's presence is quietly changing what people are willing to say in the room.

## 17. Rapid context-transfer mentoring (PF 3)

Engineers in a services model rotate across client engagements far more often than engineers in a single-product org stay on one codebase. Mentoring here frequently means compressing what a new or rotating engineer needs to know about an unfamiliar client codebase, stack, or domain into a short ramp-up window — not developing them slowly over a stable, multi-year tenure. This is a distinct skill from general mentoring: it's about triaging what matters most for near-term productive, billable contribution, not covering everything eventually.

**You need this when:** someone is joining your account next week and needs to be useful within days, not months.

## 18. Mentoring under utilization pressure (PF 3)

Mentoring time is often not billable, and it competes directly with chargeable work — which means it's the first thing that gets deprioritized when utilization targets are tight. Sustaining a mentoring relationship in a services company means actively protecting that time (blocking it, naming it explicitly to a Staff Manager or account lead) rather than assuming it will happen on its own, and being able to make the case for why the mentoring investment is worth the utilization cost.

**You need this when:** a mentoring 1:1 is the easiest thing on your calendar to cancel this week, and you're tempted to.
