# Feedback, Communication & Collaboration — Collaboration

**Competency:** Feedback, Communication & Collaboration
**Primary Function:** 3 of 3 — Collaboration
**Domain classification:** Core
**Version date:** 2026-07-06

## Scope

Helping teammates, building and leveraging relationships across a widening scope, and handling disagreement productively — from helping when requested and sharing credit at P2, up to fostering all three as an organization-wide culture at P7. Applies P2–P7. Given LFT's delivery model, "teammates" and "relationships" routinely extend across the LFT-client boundary — working inside a client's existing team structure, norms, and tooling alongside client engineers and PMs — and, for practitioners staffed across multiple concurrent client engagements, collaboration must also hold across separate client contexts without leaking one client's information or priorities into another's. Excludes the feedback-specific behaviors covered in PF 1, and the communication-specific behaviors covered in PF 2.

## Performance Criteria by Level

**P2**
- A practitioner at this level can: help teammates when requested
- A practitioner at this level can: give or share credit where due
- A practitioner at this level can: build strong relationships with teammates and manager
- A practitioner at this level can: share opinions openly and contribute to discussions in a respectful manner
- A practitioner at this level can: work with teammates to resolve disagreement in a healthy manner
- A practitioner at this level can: remain open to changing their perspective based on others' input

**P3**
- A practitioner at this level can: help teammates overcome obstacles, resolve blockers, and complete work tasks, when requested
- A practitioner at this level can: provide on-call support as determined by Engineering Management
- A practitioner at this level can: build strong relationships with teammates, manager, and their product counterpart
- A practitioner at this level can: approach disagreement with teammates non-defensively and with inquisitiveness, using contradictory opinions as a basis for constructive conversation
- A practitioner at this level can: work within a client's existing team structure, tools, and working norms (their ceremonies, ticketing conventions, communication channels) rather than defaulting to LFT-internal habits

**P4**
- A practitioner at this level can: help teammates overcome obstacles, resolve blockers, and complete work tasks, sometimes
- A practitioner at this level can: build strong relationships with teammates, manager, and their team's relevant business stakeholders
- A practitioner at this level can: encourage teammates to openly share their opinions
- A practitioner at this level can: collaborate effectively with client engineers and PMs as embedded working peers, distinguishing where client process governs from where LFT internal process governs
- A practitioner at this level can: keep context and priorities separated when staffed across more than one concurrent client engagement, without leaking one client's information into another's

**P5**
- A practitioner at this level can: help teammates overcome obstacles, resolve blockers, and complete work tasks, consistently
- A practitioner at this level can: build and improve strong relationships with teammates, manager, business stakeholders, and senior engineers across the organization
- A practitioner at this level can: leverage relationships to better plan for and position their team
- A practitioner at this level can: foster a culture within their team where people share opinions openly, approach disagreement non-defensively, and use contradictory opinions constructively
- A practitioner at this level can: coach teammates on collaborating productively inside a client's team structure, and on protecting boundaries across multiple concurrent client engagements

**P6**
- A practitioner at this level can: work across teams, consistently, to help them resolve blockers and complete work tasks
- A practitioner at this level can: ensure credit is shared where due, across teams
- A practitioner at this level can: build and improve strong relationships with engineers and managers across the organization
- A practitioner at this level can: leverage relationships to better plan for and position those teams
- A practitioner at this level can: foster this culture across several teams
- A practitioner at this level can: work through surface-level disagreements to expose the concerns of disagreeing voices
- A practitioner at this level can: set norms for how several teams collaborate across client-team boundaries and across concurrent client engagements, so individual practitioners aren't left to improvise it

**P7**
- A practitioner at this level can: work across the organization, consistently, to enable teams to support each other
- A practitioner at this level can: build and improve strong relationships across the organization
- A practitioner at this level can: leverage relationships to better plan for and position the engineering organization
- A practitioner at this level can: foster this culture across the organization
- A practitioner at this level can: integrate disagreeing perspectives from across the company into their own perspective and plans

## Required Knowledge

1. **Psychological safety and healthy-conflict basics** (why disagreement handled well strengthens a team rather than damaging it) — the foundation for the entire Handling Disagreement row, from "resolves disagreement healthily" at P2 to fostering that culture at P5+.
2. **Non-defensive communication and active inquiry techniques** (approaching a challenge to your idea with curiosity rather than defensiveness) — needed specifically for the P3 delta, where "resolving disagreement" becomes the more specific skill of approaching it "non-defensively and with inquisitiveness."
3. **Credit attribution norms** (why visibly crediting others' contributions matters for team trust, and why its absence erodes it) — a recurring, easy-to-overlook thread in the Teamwork row (P2, and again as an "ensure" at P6).
4. **On-call and incident-response fundamentals** — needed concretely for the P3–P5 on-call support expectation; this is a distinct operational skill, not just "being helpful."
5. **Relationship-building and stakeholder-mapping principles** (identifying who matters to your work and investing deliberately, not just relying on proximity) — needed for the Relationship Building row's steady widening of scope from teammates to the whole organization.
6. **Culture-building mechanics** (how a personal habit becomes a team, cross-team, or organizational norm) — shared with PF 1 and PF 2's parallel Required Knowledge items; P5–P7 PCs across all three rows in this PF are about fostering culture, not just personal behavior.
7. **Working inside a client's team structure** (adapting to a client's existing ceremonies, tooling, and decision-making norms rather than imposing LFT-internal habits) — needed from P2 onward, since embedded/staff-aug engineers routinely operate as guests inside a client's team, not just their own LFT team.
8. **Multi-engagement collaboration hygiene** (keeping context, information, and priorities separated across concurrent client engagements) — needed for P4+, where a practitioner may be staffed across more than one client at once and mishandling this is a confidentiality and trust risk, not just a scheduling inconvenience.

## Required Skills

- Proactively helping teammates without being asked (P4+)
- On-call/incident response practice
- Deliberate relationship-building and stakeholder management
- Non-defensive conflict navigation and facilitation
- Coaching others' collaboration habits and designing team/cross-team collaboration norms (P5+)
- Adapting to a client team's existing norms and tooling rather than defaulting to LFT-internal habits (P2+)
- Keeping context and information boundaries clean across multiple concurrent client engagements (P4+)

## Evidence Guide

**Critical aspects (non-negotiable):**
- Helping behavior is visible in real instances (a blocker actually resolved, on-call support actually provided), not just a stated willingness to help
- Relationships are evidenced by an actual interaction history or outcome (something planned or resolved because of the relationship), not just a claimed connection
- Disagreement-handling is evidenced by a specific instance where a real disagreement was navigated productively, not a general philosophy about conflict — this includes P2's "remains open to changing perspective," which should show up as a concrete moment where input from someone else actually changed a stated position, not just a claimed open-mindedness
- Collaboration inside a client's team structure is evidenced by a specific instance of adapting to the client's process or tooling (not LFT's default), not a general claim of being "easy to work with"
- Multi-engagement boundary-keeping (P4+) is evidenced by a concrete instance of information or context correctly kept separate between engagements, not just a statement that confidentiality is respected

**Assessment methods by level:**
- P2–P4: structured conversation about a time they helped a teammate, a relationship they've built, and a disagreement they navigated, cross-checked against any available corroboration (peer feedback, on-call records)
- P5–P6: portfolio (evidence of a collaboration culture forming — a relationship leveraged for the team, a disagreement worked through at the surface level) + a peer/manager observation
- P7: portfolio + a teaching/culture demonstration

**Work products that demonstrate this PF:** on-call incident records, peer feedback mentioning collaboration, documented instances of cross-team relationship-building (joint planning docs, shared initiatives), retrospective notes describing a disagreement and its resolution, evidence of working within a client's ceremonies/tooling (e.g. contributions in the client's own ticketing system or standups), records showing engagement-boundary discipline across concurrent clients.

## Hiring Signals

- **P2:** describes a time they helped a teammate when asked, and a time they gave credit to someone else for shared work; describes adapting to a client team's existing way of working rather than expecting the client to adapt to them, if they've worked on a client-facing engagement.
- **P3:** describes a disagreement they navigated with a teammate, approaching it with curiosity rather than defensiveness; describes their on-call experience if applicable.
- **P4:** describes proactively helping a teammate without being asked, and a time they encouraged a quieter teammate to share an opinion; describes collaborating with client engineers/PMs as embedded peers, or — if staffed across multiple engagements — how they kept those contexts appropriately separate.
- **P5:** describes a relationship with a stakeholder or senior engineer they built deliberately and later leveraged for their team's benefit; describes fostering a healthier disagreement norm on their team.
- **P6/P7:** not assessed via live scenario — see Evidence Guide (portfolio + teaching/culture demonstration).

## Promotion Criteria

- **P2 → P3:** from helping when asked and sharing credit, to consistently helping with obstacles/blockers/tasks when requested, taking on formal on-call support, and approaching disagreement with a specific non-defensive, inquisitive stance rather than just "resolving it healthily" in general terms.
- **P3 → P4:** from helping when requested, to sometimes helping proactively; from sharing one's own opinions well, to encouraging teammates to share theirs; relationship scope widens to the team's business stakeholders; and collaboration extends to working as an embedded peer inside a client's team structure, with boundary discipline if staffed across more than one client.
- **P4 → P5:** from sometimes helping, to consistently helping; from personally building relationships, to leveraging them for the team and building them across the organization; from encouraging opinion-sharing, to fostering a full disagreement-handling culture on the team; from personally navigating the client-team boundary, to coaching others to do the same.
- **P5 → P6:** from team-level helping and culture, to cross-team helping, ensuring credit is shared across teams, and fostering disagreement culture — plus a new skill of working through surface-level disagreements to expose real concerns, and setting norms for client-boundary and multi-engagement collaboration across several teams.
- **P6 → P7:** from cross-team, to enabling organization-wide mutual support, organization-wide relationships and culture, and integrating disagreeing perspectives from across the company into one's own plans.
