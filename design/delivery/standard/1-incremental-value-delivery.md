# Delivery — Incremental Value Delivery

**Competency:** Delivery
**Primary Function:** 1 of 2 — Incremental Value Delivery
**Domain classification:** Core
**Version date:** 2026-07-01

## Scope

Scoping, sizing, and sequencing work so it ships in small increments, and deciding responsibly when the full picture isn't available. Covers task/epic/initiative breakdown, priority-setting, dependency management, and navigating ambiguity — from following someone else's priority order at P2 up to installing organization-wide preventative structure at P7. Applies P2–P7. Excludes personal delivery reliability and cost/value judgment (see PF 2 — Self-Organization), which LFT's matrix treats as a distinct, more individually-scoped cluster of behaviors.

**Services context:** "the team's priority order" (1.7), "the epic/project" (1.4), and "organization-wide initiative" (1.6/1.12) are frequently set or constrained by a client — a client PM, product owner, or account manager, not a single internal backlog owner. Sizing and dependency work at LFT routinely happens against a signed SOW: a task that looks "oversized" may in fact be a fixed-bid line item, and splitting it changes what's billable and what isn't. "Dependencies" (1.8, 1.13) include client-side ones — a client team's API, a client sign-off, a client-provided dataset or environment — which are slower to resolve and less visible than an internal teammate's dependency. Engineers should also expect to size and sequence work on an engagement whose codebase/domain they ramped onto weeks or months ago, not one they've owned since inception; unfamiliarity with the client's system is itself a source of ambiguity to be triaged (see 1.13/1.14), not a reason to defer sizing indefinitely.

## Performance Criteria by Level

**P2**
- A practitioner at this level can: explain why a given task should be split before starting it, when asked
- A practitioner at this level can: work on tasks in the priority order set by the team

**P3**
- A practitioner at this level can: size a task for incremental delivery before starting it, with input from a teammate or manager
- A practitioner at this level can: note a task's dependencies before starting it
- A practitioner at this level can: make a reasonable decision on a task despite missing information, within their own scope of work

**P4**
- A practitioner at this level can: resize a task that isn't appropriately scoped for incremental delivery, identified during planning, unaided
- A practitioner at this level can: correct a task's priority and dependency notes during planning, unaided
- A practitioner at this level can: make a reasonable decision under incomplete information in both routine and high-pressure situations

**P5**
- A practitioner at this level can: break down an epic or project into well-scoped, prioritized pieces that the team understands
- A practitioner at this level can: foster a team culture of priority-setting and urgency aligned with organizational strategy
- A practitioner at this level can: guide the team through a decision made under incomplete information

**P6**
- A practitioner at this level can: break down cross-team work into pieces that all involved teams understand
- A practitioner at this level can: foster a cross-team culture of priority-setting and urgency, with dependencies understood by all teams involved
- A practitioner at this level can: guide several teams through risk, change, or uncertainty spanning their work

**P7**
- A practitioner at this level can: break down an organization-wide initiative into pieces that are prioritized and understood across the organization
- A practitioner at this level can: install an organization-wide preventative measure that stops a recurring cross-team dependency issue
- A practitioner at this level can: guide the organization through risk, change, or uncertainty at organizational scope

## Required Knowledge

1. **Incremental delivery principles** (why small, shippable increments reduce risk versus big-bang delivery) — the foundational rationale a P2 needs in order to explain "why split this task" at all.
2. **Task/epic sizing heuristics** (e.g. splitting patterns, right-sizing criteria) — without a shared vocabulary for "well-sized," sizing conversations become subjective arguments instead of a teachable skill.
3. **Dependency mapping** (how to identify and represent what blocks what) — unnoted dependencies are one of the most common causes of missed deadlines.
4. **Decision-making under uncertainty** (when "good enough" beats waiting for more data) — Dealing with Ambiguity specifically requires knowing when to act despite an incomplete picture, which is a decision-theory skill, not just nerve.
5. **Risk categorization** (which unknowns are cheap to resolve versus expensive to leave unresolved) — not all ambiguity is equally dangerous; senior engineers need to triage it rather than treat all uncertainty the same.
6. **Organizational strategy alignment** (how team-level priorities should trace up to company goals) — P5+ "fostering priority-setting aligned with organizational strategy" requires actually knowing what that strategy is, not just having good instincts. In a services context this includes the account/engagement strategy for the specific client (renewal, expansion, fixed-bid margin) alongside LFT's internal goals — the two aren't the same thing and can pull in different directions.
7. **Preventative process design** (root-causing a recurring problem versus patching each instance) — P7's "installs preventative measures" is a systems-thinking skill distinct from solving the immediate case.
8. **SOW / change-request discipline** (how scope commitments in a Statement of Work translate into sizing and priority decisions, and how a client-driven scope change gets routed through a change request rather than absorbed silently) — sizing and reprioritization decisions that ignore contract structure create billing and delivery risk even when the engineering judgment is otherwise sound.
9. **Engagement ramp-up and transition** (breaking down and sequencing work on a codebase/domain the engineer did not build, and handing off cleanly when an engagement ends or rotates staff) — services engineers size and sequence work under a different starting-knowledge condition than an engineer who has owned a product since inception; ramp speed and handoff quality are themselves part of this competency, not a separate skill.

## Required Skills

- Backlog/ticket tooling proficiency (writing clear, appropriately-sized tickets)
- Facilitation for planning conversations — breakdown sessions, dependency-mapping workshops (P5+)
- Cross-team negotiation for shared roadmaps (P6+)
- Written communication for organization-wide initiative breakdowns (P7)

## Evidence Guide

**Critical aspects (non-negotiable):**
- Tasks are sized so they can ship incrementally, not handled as one large undifferentiated block
- Dependencies are identified and noted before they cause a blocker, not discovered after
- Decisions made under incomplete information have a documented rationale, not just a silent guess

**Assessment methods by level:**
- P2–P3: guided planning exercise, plus a retrospective look at real tickets they've sized
- P4: live, unaided planning scenario (an under-scoped task or small ambiguous problem)
- P5–P6: portfolio (an epic or cross-team breakdown they drove) + live facilitation demo
- P7: portfolio + teaching demonstration

**Work products that demonstrate this PF:** sized tickets/tasks, epic breakdown documents, dependency maps (including client-side dependencies), decision logs for ambiguous calls, roadmap documents, SOW/change-request records showing a scope call was routed correctly rather than absorbed silently.

## Hiring Signals

- **P2:** can explain why a given oversized task should be broken up; describes following a priority order set by someone else.
- **P3:** describes a task they sized themselves with help; describes a decision they made without the full picture, within their own scope.
- **P4:** live scenario — given a vague, oversized task, resizes it and notes dependencies unaided; describes navigating incomplete information under real pressure, not just a routine case.
- **P5:** describes an epic they broke down and got team alignment on; describes guiding a team decision made under uncertainty.
- **P6/P7:** not assessed via live scenario — see Evidence Guide (portfolio + teaching demonstration).

## Promotion Criteria

- **P2 → P3:** from following others' prioritization and explaining sizing conceptually, to sizing tasks and noting dependencies with help, plus making small decisions under ambiguity within personal scope.
- **P3 → P4:** from guided sizing, to unaided resizing and dependency correction, and handling ambiguity under high-pressure situations, not just routine ones.
- **P4 → P5:** from individual task-level scoping, to breaking down whole epics/projects and fostering team-wide prioritization culture, guiding team decisions under uncertainty.
- **P5 → P6:** from team scope, to cross-team breakdown and culture-fostering, guiding several teams through uncertainty.
- **P6 → P7:** from cross-team, to organization-wide breakdown, installing preventative structural fixes, and guiding the whole organization through uncertainty.
