# Delivery — Self-Organization

**Competency:** Delivery
**Primary Function:** 2 of 2 — Self-Organization
**Domain classification:** Core
**Version date:** 2026-07-01

## Scope

Managing personal — and eventually team- and organization-scale — delivery reliability and cost/value judgment. Covers proactive communication of progress and blockers, realistic commitment-setting, roadmap ownership, and weighing cost against value in decisions. Applies P2–P7. Excludes the collaborative scoping/sequencing work covered in PF 1 — Incremental Value Delivery, which LFT's matrix treats as a distinct, more collaborative cluster of behaviors.

**Services context:** "the team" that progress and blockers are reported to (2.1–2.4) often sits alongside a client PM or account manager who has independent visibility and expectations — status communication frequently has two audiences, not one. Self-organization in a services engagement usually means operating well within ceremonies, tooling, and reporting cadence the client has dictated (a client's own Jira workflow, a client-mandated standup time, a client-required weekly status format), not designing that process from scratch — navigating effectively inside externally-imposed constraints, and managing up to both the LFT lead and the client stakeholder, is what self-organization looks like here. "Roadmap" (2.6, 2.7) at P6/P7 should be read as spanning multiple concurrent client engagements or accounts, not a single internal product roadmap. Cost/value judgment (2.8–2.14) is shaped by contract type: on a fixed-bid engagement, "value" includes margin and scope discipline against the SOW; on T&M, it includes client trust that hours billed map to value delivered.

## Performance Criteria by Level

**P2**
- A practitioner at this level can: report daily progress on assigned work to the team
- A practitioner at this level can: deliver on a committed task with visible urgency
- A practitioner at this level can: ask a senior engineer for help weighing cost against value on a real decision

**P3**
- A practitioner at this level can: escalate a blocker, delay, or cost overrun to the team daily, as soon as it's identified
- A practitioner at this level can: weigh cost against value on a real decision, with senior engineer input

**P4**
- A practitioner at this level can: communicate a blocker, delay, or cost overrun before it requires escalation
- A practitioner at this level can: weigh cost against value on a real decision, unaided
- A practitioner at this level can: suggest an economic tradeoff to a teammate on their work

**P5**
- A practitioner at this level can: clarify delivery expectations between the team and external stakeholders before a project starts
- A practitioner at this level can: foster a team culture of applying economic thinking to make timely decisions

**P6**
- A practitioner at this level can: manage a cross-team roadmap to delivery, with blockers anticipated and communicated across teams
- A practitioner at this level can: foster an economic-thinking culture across several teams

**P7**
- A practitioner at this level can: manage an organization-wide roadmap to delivery, with expectations clarified across the organization and with external stakeholders
- A practitioner at this level can: foster an economic-thinking culture across the organization

## Required Knowledge

1. **Commitment reliability principles** (why consistent delivery builds trust and predictability for a team) — explains why "daily conversation about progress" matters even when nothing is wrong, not just when something is.
2. **Escalation versus anticipation** (raising an issue after it's a problem versus before) — this is exactly the P3→P4 delta: knowing when something is escalation-worthy before it becomes urgent.
3. **Cost/value tradeoff frameworks** (e.g. opportunity cost, cost of delay, sunk-cost reasoning) — "weighing cost against value" is a vague instruction without a concrete framework to actually apply it with.
4. **Stakeholder expectation-setting** (why clarifying scope and timeline upfront prevents downstream conflict) — P5's "clarify delivery expectations... before a project starts" is a preventative communication skill, not a reactive one. In a services engagement this typically means clarifying expectations with the client PM/account manager and, where relevant, tying the conversation to what the SOW actually commits to — not just an internal team agreement.
5. **Roadmap management at scale** (how commitments, dependencies, and risk aggregate across many workstreams) — P6/P7 are managing portfolios of commitments, which requires different thinking than tracking a single project. At LFT this typically means several concurrent client engagements with different contract types, cadences, and reporting expectations, rather than one product roadmap.
6. **Navigating externally-imposed process constraints** (staying reliable and proactive when the client, not the engineer or their manager, sets the ceremonies, tooling, and reporting cadence) — self-organization under a client-dictated process is a distinct skill from designing one's own process, and is the more common condition on client engagements.

## Required Skills

- Status reporting / standup communication
- Written stakeholder communication for setting and clarifying expectations
- Portfolio/roadmap tracking at scale (P6+)
- Coaching others in economic reasoning (P5+)

## Evidence Guide

**Critical aspects (non-negotiable):**
- Progress and blockers are communicated proactively, not only when someone asks
- Commitments made are realistic, and are met or visibly renegotiated before they're missed — not silently missed
- Cost/value tradeoffs are explicit and explainable, not just gut feel presented as a decision

**Assessment methods by level:**
- P2–P4: manager/peer retrospective on a recent delivery (checking the track record), plus a walkthrough of one specific cost/value decision
- P5–P6: portfolio (a stakeholder expectation-setting example or a cross-team roadmap)
- P7: portfolio + teaching demonstration

**Work products that demonstrate this PF:** standup/status notes, escalation records, roadmap documents, decision write-ups that show cost/value reasoning, client-facing status reports, change-request/scope-negotiation records.

## Hiring Signals

- **P2:** describes a recent task they delivered and how they communicated progress along the way.
- **P3:** describes a blocker they escalated, and explains when and why they raised it.
- **P4:** describes a blocker they caught and communicated before it needed escalation; describes an economic tradeoff they made unaided.
- **P5:** describes a stakeholder expectation-setting conversation they led before a project started.
- **P6/P7:** not assessed via live scenario — see Evidence Guide (portfolio + teaching demonstration).

## Promotion Criteria

- **P2 → P3:** from reporting progress when prompted, to proactively escalating issues daily as they're identified.
- **P3 → P4:** from escalating after identifying an issue, to anticipating and communicating before escalation is needed; from needing senior input on cost/value, to weighing it unaided.
- **P4 → P5:** from individual reliability, to clarifying expectations with external stakeholders and fostering team-wide economic thinking.
- **P5 → P6:** from team-level roadmap and culture, to managing cross-team roadmaps and economic-thinking culture.
- **P6 → P7:** from cross-team, to organization-wide roadmap management and economic-thinking culture.
