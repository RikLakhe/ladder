# Strategic Impact — Business Acumen & Strategy

**Competency:** Strategic Impact
**Primary Function:** 1 of 1 — Business Acumen & Strategy
**Domain classification:** Core
**Version date:** 2026-07-02

## Scope

Understanding the business context engineering work sits in — **primarily the client's business**, since LFT is a services/consulting organization delivering to enterprise clients rather than owning a product P&L — participating in and eventually shaping engineering strategy, and applying product/account judgment to roadmap, feature, and account-growth decisions. Covers client-domain/market awareness, contract-economics awareness (T&M vs. fixed-bid margin implications, utilization), strategic participation, account growth (spotting expansion and cross-sell opportunities, becoming a trusted advisor an account renews and expands with), and product thinking as one cluster — from explaining a client's product's basic utility at P2, up to shaping strategy across a portfolio of client accounts with LFT's account/business counterparts at P7. Practitioners at every level influence the client's technical direction as a trusted advisor; they do not own the client's product roadmap or P&L. Applies P2–P7.

## Performance Criteria by Level

**P2**
- A practitioner at this level can: describe how their team's domain fits into the client account's business and the service LFT provides to it, when asked
- A practitioner at this level can: explain the basic utility of the client's product they work on

**P3**
- A practitioner at this level can: explain how their team's domain contributes to the client account's business goals and to LFT's overall business strategy
- A practitioner at this level can: explain the organization's engineering strategy, when asked
- A practitioner at this level can: explain how their product area of focus fits into the client's business, suggesting an improvement to it when relevant
- A practitioner at this level can: explain, at a basic level, whether their engagement is T&M or fixed-bid and what that implies for how they scope and estimate their own work

**P4**
- A practitioner at this level can: apply a thorough understanding of their team's domain, plus a basic understanding of adjacent teams' domains, to a real engineering decision
- A practitioner at this level can: participate in a discussion about what the organization's engineering strategy implies for their team
- A practitioner at this level can: explain the business model — including the account's contract type (T&M vs. fixed-bid) and its effect on margin and scoping decisions — in relation to their current project focus area
- A practitioner at this level can: participate in roadmap feedback with the project team, sometimes
- A practitioner at this level can: identify an opportunity to simplify project or technical design
- A practitioner at this level can: spot and raise a potential expansion or improvement opportunity within the current client account, even if it isn't acted on

**P5**
- A practitioner at this level can: connect their team's domain, strategy, and market trends to a real decision, including a thorough understanding of adjacent teams' strategies
- A practitioner at this level can: decide on the team's engineering work based on the organization's engineering strategy, collaboratively with the team
- A practitioner at this level can: contribute to organizational engineering strategy work, when invited
- A practitioner at this level can: evaluate or create a new product feature in collaboration with the client's product team, giving a technical recommendation that accounts for the engagement's contract economics (scope, margin, utilization impact)
- A practitioner at this level can: participate regularly in the creation of the team/account roadmap
- A practitioner at this level can: simplify product or technical design through a proactive conversation
- A practitioner at this level can: identify a concrete account-growth opportunity (an expansion of scope, a cross-sell of an adjacent LFT capability, or additional work the client would value) and raise it with their manager or account lead

**P6**
- A practitioner at this level can: apply a thorough understanding of several teams' domains — potentially spanning more than one client account — to a cross-team business decision
- A practitioner at this level can: lead a cross-team strategic effort that achieves cross-team alignment on a major goal
- A practitioner at this level can: participate in a strategic organizational decision or plan
- A practitioner at this level can: recognize a product opportunity or a differentiator relative to alternative approaches or vendors the client is weighing, and act as the client's trusted technical advisor in that judgment
- A practitioner at this level can: refine a roadmap across teams based on technical strategy and constraints
- A practitioner at this level can: shape an account growth plan — identifying and acting on cross-sell, expansion, or renewal opportunities across one or more client accounts

**P7**
- A practitioner at this level can: apply a thorough understanding of the entire business — all domains, client accounts, and their contribution to strategy — to an organization-wide decision
- A practitioner at this level can: lead a strategic organizational decision or plan
- A practitioner at this level can: influence a decision to achieve organization-wide alignment on a major goal
- A practitioner at this level can: create or redefine a roadmap across the organization with product and business counterparts
- A practitioner at this level can: influence strategy and account growth across a portfolio of several client accounts, not just one, acting as a strategic advisor clients renew and expand with

## Required Knowledge

1. **Client business model literacy** (the *client's* revenue drivers, cost structure, and market position — not LFT's, since LFT doesn't own the client's product) — without this, "understanding the business" stays a slogan; a P3+ engineer needs to know what actually makes the client's business work to connect their domain to strategy meaningfully.
2. **Contract-economics literacy** (T&M vs. fixed-bid engagement structure, how scope creep or gold-plating affects margin, what utilization means and why it matters to the account) — the services-company equivalent of "business model" knowledge; without it, "understanding the business model" collapses into generic product theory that doesn't fit how LFT actually gets paid.
3. **Company and engineering strategy literacy** (what LFT's actual stated strategy is, not a generic guess) — see `../../my-role.md` and `../../goals/company-okr-2026.md` for the concrete artifacts P3+ PCs should trace to; "explaining the organization's engineering strategy" requires there to be a real strategy to explain.
4. **Market and competitive awareness** (who else the client could have chosen — other vendors, in-house teams, alternative approaches — and how LFT's work compares) — needed specifically for P5's "market trends" and P6's "differentiator" language; without it those PCs collapse into generic opinion.
5. **Product management fundamentals** (roadmap structures, prioritization frameworks, feature evaluation criteria) — Product Thinking PCs from P4 up assume the practitioner can participate in, not just observe, the client's product conversations as an advisor, not as the product owner.
6. **Account growth and farming awareness** (how to spot expansion opportunities, cross-sell adjacent LFT capabilities, and become an advisor a client renews and expands with) — the services-company analogue of "product opportunity" recognition; P4+ PCs assume the practitioner can see and raise these opportunities, not just execute assigned scope.
7. **Organizational influence without formal authority** (how decisions actually get made and changed at LFT, and — separately — how to influence a client's technical direction without owning their roadmap) — P5–P7's "collaborate," "lead," and "influence" language is meaningless without knowing how influence actually works in this specific organization and with client counterparts, not in the abstract.
8. **Strategic communication** (translating technical tradeoffs into business terms and vice versa) — the throughline connecting every level: this competency is fundamentally about translating between "what the code does" and "why it matters to the client's business," and that's a communication skill, not just a knowledge one.

## Required Skills

- Cross-functional collaboration with the client's product and business counterparts, as a trusted advisor rather than the business owner
- Roadmap facilitation and feature-evaluation judgment, contract-economics-aware (P5+)
- Cross-team, cross-account, and organizational influence-building (P6+)
- Market/competitive analysis, framed around the client's alternatives (vendors, in-house build, other approaches) rather than LFT's own market position (P5+)
- Spotting and acting on account-growth opportunities — expansion, cross-sell, renewal — within and, at P6/P7, across client accounts
- Translating technical constraints into business-legible tradeoffs, and business goals into technical implications

## Evidence Guide

**Critical aspects (non-negotiable):**
- Business/strategy understanding shows up applied to a real decision, not just recited when asked
- Strategic participation is a documented contribution (a decision shaped, a plan influenced), not just attendance at a meeting where strategy was discussed
- Product judgment is visible in an artifact — a suggestion, a roadmap note, a feature spec — not just an opinion stated in conversation

**Assessment methods by level:**
- P2–P4: structured conversation covering domain/business understanding, strategy awareness, and product judgment, cross-checked against one real artifact (a decision, a suggestion, or roadmap feedback they actually gave)
- P5–P6: portfolio (a strategic contribution or a roadmap they helped shape) + a collaboration/facilitation observation
- P7: portfolio + a strategy teaching/communication demonstration

**Work products that demonstrate this PF:** roadmap documents, feature specs, strategy discussion notes, decision write-ups that reference business/market context, contract-aware technical recommendations, client advisory memos, account-growth plans, cross-team or org-wide alignment plans.

## Hiring Signals

- **P2:** can describe what the client's product does and why it matters to a user, in plain terms.
- **P3:** explains how their team's work connects to a specific client business goal; describes a product improvement they suggested; can say whether their engagement is T&M or fixed-bid and why that matters.
- **P4:** describes a technical decision that changed once they understood the client's business model or the engagement's contract economics better; describes an account-growth or expansion opportunity they noticed and raised.
- **P5:** describes a product feature they helped shape with the client's product team as a trusted advisor (not as the decision owner), and how it connected to team or account strategy; describes a contract-economics tradeoff (scope, margin, utilization) they factored into a recommendation.
- **P6/P7:** not assessed via live scenario — see Evidence Guide (portfolio + teaching/communication demonstration). At this level, expect the portfolio to show influence across more than one client account, not just one.

## Promotion Criteria

- **P2 → P3:** from describing domain/product basics when asked, to explaining how that domain connects to the client's business strategy and making occasional improvement suggestions unprompted.
- **P3 → P4:** from explaining strategy when asked, to applying domain and contract-economics understanding to real decisions, participating in strategy-implication discussions and roadmap feedback for their team, and spotting account-expansion opportunities.
- **P4 → P5:** from participating in team-level strategy discussions, to deciding on team engineering work based on org strategy, contributing to organizational strategy work when invited, actively shaping the client's product features and roadmap as a trusted advisor, and surfacing concrete account-growth opportunities.
- **P5 → P6:** from team-level strategic participation, to applying cross-team (and potentially cross-account) domain understanding, leading cross-team strategic efforts, refining roadmaps across teams, and shaping an account-growth plan.
- **P6 → P7:** from participating in strategic organizational decisions, to leading them — from refining cross-team roadmaps to redefining roadmaps across the whole organization with business counterparts — and from single-account growth influence to shaping strategy and growth across a portfolio of client accounts.
