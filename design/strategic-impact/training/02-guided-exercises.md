# Strategic Impact — Guided Exercises

Five exercises, ordered simplest to most complex, covering P2–P4. Each maps to specific sub-functions from `../functional-analysis.md` **and** to the badge(s) it builds evidence toward — see `05-badge-reference-card.md` for the full map. From P5 up, the standard's own Evidence Guide moves to portfolio + real-work observation, so there is no guided exercise beyond Exercise 5 — see `03-autonomous-projects.md`.

None of these exercises award a badge by themselves. Completing one produces (or rehearses producing) the artifact a badge's instrument requires. The badge itself still needs the named Verifier's sign-off per `../assessment/badges.md` §1.3.

Each exercise depends only on exercises before it in this file, plus the concept notes it cites — no forward dependencies.

## Exercise 1 — Client-context narration (domain-to-account mapping)

**Maps to sub-functions:** 1.1, 1.15 [P2]
**Builds toward badge:** SI-1-P2 (Client Context Narrator)
**Instrument this rehearses:** `../assessment/pf1-business-acumen-strategy/01-work-product-review-rubric.md` — "Business/domain understanding applied" and "Product judgment & account awareness" rows at Meets P2; `02-live-demo-checklist.md` P2 items
**Prerequisite:** Concept notes §1 (Client business model literacy)

**Goal:** Produce a short, accurate description — in your own words, not a memorized script — of what your team's domain does inside the client account, and what the client's product is actually for.

**Setup:** No special setup needed beyond your current assignment. Pick one real ticket, onboarding note, or feature you worked on in the last two weeks as your reference point.

**Step-by-step:**
1. Write two sentences: (a) what does the client's product do for the people who use it? (b) what does your team specifically build or maintain inside that product, and what service does LFT provide to this account?
2. Pick the one real reference point (ticket, onboarding note, or a walkthrough you gave someone) that best illustrates (b). Note why it's a good example.
3. Say both sentences out loud to a buddy or your manager, without reading from notes. Ask them: "does this sound like something you memorized, or something you actually understand?"
4. Revise anything that sounded recited rather than understood.

**Expected output:** two short, spoken-and-revised sentences, plus one named reference point (ticket/note/walkthrough) that backs sentence (b).

**Check:** can you answer a follow-up question ("why does that feature matter to the client's business, not just the user?") without pausing to reconstruct an answer from scratch? If yes, you're ready for the structured conversation; if you had to think hard, revisit concept notes §1 and try a different reference point.

**If evidence can't leave the client's environment:** the reference point doesn't need to leave anywhere for this exercise — the check is conversational, not a document review. This matters more from Exercise 3 onward.

## Exercise 2 — Strategy translation + contract-type drill

**Maps to sub-functions:** 1.2, 1.7, 1.16 [P3]
**Builds toward badge:** SI-1-P3 (Strategy Translator)
**Instrument this rehearses:** `02-live-demo-checklist.md` P3 items, including the T&M/fixed-bid item; `01-work-product-review-rubric.md` "Strategic awareness & participation" row at Meets P3
**Prerequisite:** Exercise 1; Concept notes §2–3 (Contract-economics, Company & engineering strategy literacy)

**Goal:** Explain, unprompted, how your domain connects to the client account's business goals and to LFT's strategy — and state your engagement's actual contract type with one concrete scoping implication.

**Setup:** Have `../../my-role.md` and `../../goals/company-okr-2026.md` open. If you don't already know your engagement's contract type, ask your manager or delivery lead before starting — this is a factual question with a real answer, not something to infer.

**Step-by-step:**
1. Read (or re-read) the relevant sections of `my-role.md` and `company-okr-2026.md`. Write one sentence naming a specific, current LFT strategic priority (not a generic guess).
2. Write one sentence connecting your team's domain to a specific client business goal (not "we help the client succeed" — name the actual goal).
3. Confirm your engagement's contract type (T&M or fixed-bid) and write one concrete implication it has for how you scope or estimate your own work (e.g. "on this fixed-bid engagement, I flag anything that looks like scope creep before starting it, because it eats margin directly").
4. Practice saying all three, unaided, to a buddy — no notes visible. Have them ask a follow-up ("why does that matter, not just what is it") on each.

**Expected output:** three spoken-and-revised statements (strategy connection, business-goal connection, contract-type implication), able to survive a follow-up question without notes.

**Check:** could you give a different, equally correct example if your buddy asked "give me another example of a scoping decision that contract type affects"? If you can only produce the one rehearsed example, the understanding isn't solid yet — go back to concept notes §2.

## Exercise 3 — Product-suggestion & roadmap-feedback artifact building

**Maps to sub-functions:** 1.16, 1.18, 1.19 [P3–P4]
**Builds toward badges:** SI-1-P3 (product-improvement-suggestion row) and partially SI-1-P4 (roadmap-feedback and simplification-opportunity items)
**Instrument this rehearses:** `01-work-product-review-rubric.md` "Product judgment & account awareness" row, Meets P3 and Meets P4 columns
**Prerequisite:** Exercise 2; Concept notes §5 (Product management fundamentals)

**Goal:** Produce a real, documented artifact — not a hypothetical — showing you suggested a product improvement and gave roadmap feedback or identified a simplification opportunity.

**Setup:** This exercise runs against your actual current work, over roughly 1–2 weeks, not in a single sitting.

**Step-by-step:**
1. Over your next few work sessions, watch for one place where the current design, scope, or feature choice seems more complex than the underlying need requires.
2. Write the improvement suggestion or simplification idea as a real ticket comment, doc note, or Slack message to the project team — not a private note to yourself. Use one sentence to state the current approach, one to state your suggested alternative, and one to state why (grounded in a real cost: time, complexity, risk, or client value — not just personal preference).
3. If your team has a roadmap discussion or planning session in this window, contribute one piece of feedback in it, and note afterward (to yourself) what you said and whether it was taken up.
4. Collect the artifact (ticket comment, doc link, or meeting note) somewhere you can retrieve it later — this is the exact artifact the P3/P4 rubric rows ask for.

**Expected output:** one real, dated, retrievable artifact (ticket/doc/message) documenting a product-improvement suggestion, plus (if the timing allowed) a documented instance of roadmap feedback.

**Check:** if your manager asked you right now "show me the last product suggestion you made," could you produce the link in under a minute? If not, the artifact isn't documented well enough yet — go back and add it somewhere retrievable.

**If a roadmap session didn't happen in your window:** that's fine — don't manufacture one. Note it and pick this back up when the next one occurs; this doesn't block moving on to Exercise 4, since Exercise 4 doesn't depend on this item being complete.

## Exercise 4 — Contract-aware decision case study

**Maps to sub-functions:** 1.3, 1.17 [P4]
**Builds toward badge:** SI-1-P4 (Contract-Aware Decision Driver) — the decision item specifically
**Instrument this rehearses:** `01-work-product-review-rubric.md` "Business/domain understanding applied" row, Meets P4; `02-live-demo-checklist.md` P4 item
**Prerequisite:** Exercise 2 (contract-type drill); Concept notes §4 (Market/competitive awareness)

**Goal:** Produce one documented, real decision where your understanding of the business model, contract economics, or an adjacent team's domain changed the approach you took — not just informed it in the abstract.

**Setup:** This is a retrospective exercise on a decision you've already made, or a forward-looking one on a decision currently in front of you. Either is valid as long as it's real.

**Step-by-step:**
1. Identify a real technical decision (architecture choice, scope call, build-vs-buy, effort estimate) from the last month where contract economics or business context was actually a factor — not decorative.
2. Write a short decision record (3–5 sentences): the decision, the contract-economics or adjacent-domain factor that mattered, what you would have done differently without that factor, and the outcome (or expected outcome, if forward-looking).
3. Check specifically: did the business/contract factor *change* the approach, or just get mentioned afterward to justify a decision you'd have made anyway? If the latter, this isn't a strong artifact yet — find a decision where it genuinely mattered, or be honest in the write-up that the influence was marginal.
4. Share the decision record with your manager or a senior colleague and ask: "does this read like the business context actually drove the outcome?"

**Expected output:** one documented decision record meeting the "explicitly changed the outcome, not just decorated it" bar from the rubric.

**Check:** could someone reading only your decision record tell what would have happened *without* the business/contract factor, and see that it's genuinely different from what happened *with* it? If the "without" case is identical to the "with" case, revise or pick a different decision.

## Exercise 5 — Account-growth opportunity spotting drill

**Maps to sub-functions:** extends 1.20/1.23 for services context [P4]
**Builds toward badge:** SI-1-P4 (the account-growth clause) — good-faith item, not non-substitutable
**Instrument this rehearses:** `01-work-product-review-rubric.md` "Product judgment & account awareness" row, Meets P4 (account-growth clause); `02-live-demo-checklist.md` P4 prompt on account-growth
**Prerequisite:** Exercise 3; Concept notes §6 (Account growth and farming awareness)

**Goal:** Build the habit of noticing and raising a genuine account-growth or expansion opportunity — not manufacturing one.

**Setup:** No special setup — this is an ongoing-attention exercise, run over the same 1–2 week window as Exercise 3 if convenient.

**Step-by-step:**
1. As you work, watch for a moment where you think "the client would probably value X, and we could plausibly deliver it" — an adjacent capability, a scope expansion, an efficiency the client hasn't asked for but would likely welcome.
2. If you notice one: write it down in 2–3 sentences (what the opportunity is, why you think the client would value it, roughly how big a lift it'd be) and raise it with your manager or account lead — a real conversation or message, not just a private note.
3. If you don't notice a genuine opportunity in this window: say so. Do not force one. Note in your own tracking that none arose — this is the exact scenario `01-work-product-review-rubric.md`'s good-faith clause and `../assessment/badges.md` §1.7 are built to handle honestly.
4. Either way, be ready to describe, in a structured conversation, what you did (raised it, or genuinely found nothing) and why.

**Expected output:** either a documented, raised opportunity (message/note + who you raised it to), or an honest, dated note that none arose during the window.

**Check:** if asked "have you ever noticed an opportunity for LFT to do more for this client," can you answer specifically — either with the real example, or with "not in this window, and here's what I was watching for"? A vague "I'm sure there's something" is not a pass on either path.
