# Functional Analysis: Strategic Impact

> [Competency index](../index.md) › [Strategic Impact](./index.md)

**Version date:** 2026-07-02

One-line purpose: an engineer who has this competency understands the business and market their work sits in, participates in and eventually shapes engineering strategy, and applies product judgment to roadmap and feature decisions — at a level appropriate to their career stage.

**Domain classification:** Core — occupation-defining for every engineering role at LFT (business/strategic awareness is a named, promotion-gating dimension at every level from P3 up), not shared incidentally (Cross-domain) and not merely a prerequisite for something else (Foundational).

**Source:** `competencies/industry-evidence/strategic-impact.md` (grounded 2026-07-02, all six levels as separate passes). Per that brief's scope note, this competency maps to the whole of Section 5 "Strategic Impact" in `competencies/lft-engineering-competency-matrix.md`, which contains one named group — "Business Acumen & Strategy" — with three rows: Business Acumen, Strategic Work, Product Thinking.

**Assessability check (done during Phase A, not deferred to Phase C):** applying the same test used for Technical Skill's PF5/6 split and Delivery's PF1/PF2 confirmation: can Business Acumen + Strategic Work + Product Thinking be watched or reviewed together in one exercise? At P2–P4, yes — a single structured conversation ("what's your product, how does your team's work connect to strategy, what have you suggested or shaped") naturally covers all three without forcing anything. At P5–P7, also yes — a single portfolio review ("show me a strategic contribution, a roadmap you shaped, and how you understand the business context around both") covers all three as one coherent review, the same way Delivery's Self-Organization PF combined Reliability and Economic Thinking into one retrospective conversation. Conclusion: keep LFT's own single-group structure as one Primary Function, rather than splitting Product Thinking out on the theory that it's artifact-reviewable while the other two are conversation-reviewable — in practice all three show up in the same conversation or the same portfolio at every level, so a split would separate things that are naturally assessed together.

## PF 1 — Business Acumen & Strategy

Understand the business and market context of engineering work, participate in and eventually shape engineering strategy, and apply product judgment to roadmap and feature decisions.

**Scope:** Domain and market awareness; participation in organizational engineering strategy; product/roadmap judgment. Single Primary Function per the assessability check above — LFT's own matrix groups all three rows under one heading, and all three are naturally reviewed together at every level.

**Note (Phase A, updated after Phase C Pass 1):** this PF runs to 25 sub-functions across 3 matrix rows (Business Acumen, Strategic Work, Product Thinking), above the methodology's "≈4–10" granularity guideline — flagged as an accepted exception, not a defect, for the same reason accepted in Technical Skill's PF 2 (12) and both of Delivery's PFs (17, 14): each row has genuinely distinct language at every level in the source matrix, with no "See P4"-style consolidation cues, and the assessability check above confirms all three rows are still watchable/reviewable as one coherent exercise despite the count. This is, so far, the largest single-PF count in the pipeline — worth noting as a pattern: LFT's matrix rows in this pipeline consistently run above the original ≈4–10 guideline once decomposed level-by-level, suggesting the guideline itself may need revisiting rather than continuing to grant one-off exceptions. Flagged for the pipeline owner, not resolved here.

Sub-functions:

*Business Acumen:*
- 1.1 [P2] Describe how their team's domain fits into the company's CI/CD marketplace, when asked
- 1.2 [P3] Explain how their team's domain contributes to the company's overall business strategy
- 1.3 [P4] Apply a thorough understanding of their team's domain, plus a basic understanding of adjacent teams' domains, to a real engineering decision
- 1.4 [P5] Connect their team's domain, strategy, and market trends to a real decision, including a thorough understanding of adjacent teams' strategies
- 1.5 [P6] Apply a thorough understanding of several teams' domains to a cross-team business decision
- 1.6 [P7] Apply a thorough understanding of the entire business — all domains and their contribution to strategy — to an organization-wide decision

*Strategic Work (no P2 — the matrix marks this explicitly "n/a" at P2; not forced):*
- 1.7 [P3] Explain the organization's engineering strategy, when asked
- 1.8 [P4] Participate in a discussion about what the organization's engineering strategy implies for their team
- 1.9 [P5] Decide on the team's engineering work based on the organization's engineering strategy, collaboratively with the team
- 1.10 [P5] Contribute to organizational engineering strategy work, when invited
- 1.11 [P6] Lead a cross-team strategic effort that achieves cross-team alignment on a major goal
- 1.12 [P6] Participate in a strategic organizational decision or plan
- 1.13 [P7] Lead a strategic organizational decision or plan
- 1.14 [P7] Influence a decision to achieve organization-wide alignment on a major goal

*Product Thinking:*
- 1.15 [P2] Explain the basic utility of the product they work on
- 1.16 [P3] Explain how their product area of focus fits into the overall business, suggesting an improvement to it when relevant
- 1.17 [P4] Explain the business model in relation to their current project focus area
- 1.18 [P4] Participate in roadmap feedback with the project team, sometimes
- 1.19 [P4] Identify an opportunity to simplify project or technical design
- 1.20 [P5] Evaluate or create a new product feature in collaboration with the product team
- 1.21 [P5] Participate regularly in the creation of the team roadmap
- 1.22 [P5] Simplify product or technical design through a proactive conversation
- 1.23 [P6] Recognize a product opportunity or competitive differentiator relative to the competition
- 1.24 [P6] Refine a roadmap across teams based on technical strategy and constraints
- 1.25 [P7] Create or redefine a roadmap across the organization with product and business counterparts

**Phase C Pass 1 correction (2026-07-02):** the original single-pass draft bundled "participate in roadmap feedback" with "identify a simplification opportunity" (P4), and "participate in roadmap creation" with "simplify design through proactive conversations" (P5), into one sub-function each — a compound-behavior violation of Gate 1 grammar (two distinct actions joined by "and"). The LFT matrix itself already treats these as separate sentences/behaviors; split back into 1.18/1.19 and 1.21/1.22 above to match. IDs 1.20–1.23 renumbered to 1.20–1.25 as a result.

## Related

- Prerequisite competencies: none formally required. Delivery's PF 1 (Incremental Value Delivery) — specifically its P5+ "foster a culture of priority-setting aligned with organizational strategy" language — overlaps in spirit with this competency's Strategic Work row, but the two reinforce rather than gate each other.
- Downstream competencies: this competency, at P6/P7 scope, is a natural input to any future "Technical Strategy" or "Engineering Leadership" competency where setting organizational technical direction becomes the primary job rather than one dimension of an engineering role.
- Cross-domain overlaps: Delivery's PF 2 "Economic Thinking" row is narrower (individual cost/value judgment on a specific decision) than this competency's Business Acumen row (broader market/business-model understanding) — flagged in Delivery's own functional-analysis.md as a forward-reference to this competency; now resolved: Strategic Impact is the intended home for the broader, strategic-level version of that judgment, and Delivery's Economic Thinking should stay scoped to decision-level cost/value reasoning rather than being re-scoped upward.
